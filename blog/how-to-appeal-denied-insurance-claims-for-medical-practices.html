How to Appeal Denied Insurance Claims for Medical Practices

Quick answer

To appeal denied insurance claims for medical practices, identify the denial reason code, gather supporting documentation, confirm payer-specific appeal deadlines, and submit a written appeal that directly addresses the denial. Track every appeal until it resolves, and escalate to a second-level or external review if the first appeal is unsuccessful.

  • Correct denial reason code identified before drafting the appeal
  • Payer-specific appeal timeline and submission method confirmed
  • Supporting clinical and billing documentation attached
  • Appeal letter addresses the specific denial reason, not a general dispute
  • A tracking system in place to monitor deadlines and outcomes

Key takeaways

  • Denials generally fall into categories such as eligibility, medical necessity, coding errors, timely filing, and missing authorization, and each category needs a different appeal approach.
  • Payer appeal windows and required formats vary by plan type, so the same denial reason can require different handling depending on the payer.
  • Many practices lose appealable revenue simply because appeals are not filed within the payer's deadline.
  • A documented, repeatable appeal workflow reduces staff time spent per claim and can make it easier to spot denial patterns over time.
  • Appeals that cite specific plan language, coding guidelines, or clinical documentation tend to be more complete than appeals that only restate the original claim.

What you need

  • The original claim, including CPT, ICD-10, and modifier data as submitted
  • The denial letter or explanation of benefits (EOB) with the specific denial reason code
  • Payer provider manual or portal access for appeal deadlines and submission requirements
  • Relevant medical record documentation supporting the service billed
  • A payer contract or fee schedule reference, if available
  • A claim tracking log or practice management system report
  • Staff time allocated to draft, submit, and follow up on the appeal

Step-by-step instructions for how to appeal denied insurance claims for medical practices

  1. Pull the denial reason code from the EOB or remittance advice and confirm what it means according to the payer's documentation. Check that the code matches what actually happened with the claim before moving forward.
  2. Compare the denial reason against the original claim data, including codes, modifiers, dates of service, and provider information. Check for any clerical errors that could be corrected with a simple resubmission instead of a formal appeal.
  3. Confirm the payer's appeal deadline and required submission channel, since some payers require portal submission while others accept mail or fax. Check that the deadline has not already passed before committing staff time.
  4. Gather supporting documentation, such as progress notes, operative reports, prior authorization records, or medical necessity documentation. Check that the records clearly connect to the denied service and date of service.
  5. Draft a written appeal letter that states the claim information, the denial reason, and a specific, documented response to that reason. Check that the letter references applicable coding guidelines or payer policy where relevant.
  6. Attach all supporting documents and submit the appeal through the payer's required method. Check that you retain a copy of everything submitted, including confirmation of receipt.
  7. Log the appeal in a tracking system with the submission date, payer response deadline, and assigned staff member. Check this log on a set schedule so follow-up never relies on memory alone.
  8. If the first-level appeal is denied, review the payer's next appeal level, which may include a second-level internal appeal or an external review. Check whether additional documentation or a peer-to-peer review is available at that stage.
General appeal levels for denied insurance claims
Appeal level Typical action
First-level appeal Written appeal submitted directly to the payer with supporting documentation
Second-level appeal Internal review by a different payer reviewer, sometimes including peer-to-peer discussion
External review Independent review outside the payer, available depending on plan type and state rules

Safety checks

  • Verify the appeal deadline before starting work, since missing it can eliminate the right to appeal entirely.
  • Confirm the patient's plan and coverage details have not changed between the date of service and the appeal, which can affect the appeal outcome.
  • Check that documentation submitted matches the actual date of service and provider, since mismatched records can cause a second denial.
  • Stop and escalate to a coding or compliance resource if the denial reason suggests a documentation or coding pattern affecting multiple claims.
  • Confirm appeal letters do not include more protected health information than necessary for the specific payer review.

Common mistakes

  • Missing the appeal deadline: this forfeits the appeal right entirely; track deadlines from the date of the denial notice, not the date staff review it.
  • Appealing without addressing the actual denial reason: a generic appeal letter is often denied again; restate and respond to the specific reason code.
  • Submitting incomplete documentation: missing records are a common reason second appeals also fail; confirm every referenced document is actually attached.
  • Not confirming the correct submission method: some payers reject appeals sent through the wrong channel; verify portal, mail, or fax requirements first.
  • Failing to track appeal status: appeals can be lost without a response if no one follows up; use a dedicated tracking log with assigned owners.
  • Treating every denial the same way: eligibility, authorization, and coding denials need different evidence; match the response to the denial category.
  • Not escalating after a second denial: some claims are abandoned after one unsuccessful appeal even when a second-level or external review is available.

Troubleshooting

What if the payer says the appeal deadline already passed?

This is often caused by delayed internal routing of the denial notice. Check the date on the original EOB versus the date staff received it, and ask the payer whether a good-cause exception applies, since some payers allow limited exceptions for documented delays.

What if the same denial reason keeps recurring across multiple claims?

This usually points to a documentation or coding pattern rather than an isolated error. Review recent claims with the same code or reason and consider a coding or documentation review to address the root cause.

What if supporting documentation does not clearly justify the service billed?

This may be a documentation gap rather than a billing error. Work with the treating provider to clarify the record, where appropriate, before resubmitting, since adding unsupported information after the fact is not appropriate.

What if the payer requests additional information after the appeal is submitted?

This is a normal part of many appeal processes. Respond within the payer's stated timeframe and keep a copy of both the request and the response for the claim file.

Limitations and checks

  • Appeal deadlines, required formats, and number of appeal levels vary by payer and plan type, and must be verified directly with the payer or in the current provider manual.
  • State rules on external review availability and timing can differ, so practices should confirm current requirements for their state and payer mix.
  • This article provides general process guidance only and does not replace a payer's official appeal policy or legal or compliance advice specific to a practice's situation.
  • Coding and documentation standards referenced during an appeal should be verified against current official coding guidance before submission.
  • Appeal outcomes cannot be assured and depend on the specific payer, plan, documentation, and denial reason involved.

For current, authoritative detail beyond this general overview, cross-check appeal requirements against the payer's own provider manual or appeals policy page, and verify any coding-specific points against the current official CPT and ICD-10 coding guideline publications before relying on them in an appeal letter.

Bottom line

A structured appeal process is the most reliable way to recover revenue from denied claims. Start by correctly identifying the denial reason, confirm the payer's specific deadline and format, and submit a written appeal supported by relevant documentation. Track every appeal until resolution and escalate unresolved claims to the next available review level. Practices that document a repeatable process and review denial patterns over time tend to spend less staff time per appeal. For ongoing denial trends, consider pairing appeal workflows with a broader denial prevention strategy and routine documentation audits to reduce future denials.

Frequently asked questions

How long do medical practices have to appeal a denied claim?

Appeal windows are set by each payer and plan, and can range from a short window to several months depending on the contract. Practices should check the specific denial letter or the payer's provider manual for the exact deadline rather than assuming a standard timeframe applies across all payers.

What information should be included in an appeal letter?

An appeal letter should include the patient and claim identifiers, the specific denial reason being addressed, and a documented response supported by medical records or coding guidance. Avoid generic language and focus the letter directly on why the original denial reason does not apply to this claim.

Can a denied claim be appealed more than once?

Yes, many payers offer multiple appeal levels, such as a first-level internal appeal followed by a second-level review or external review, depending on the plan type. Each level typically has its own deadline and documentation requirements that should be confirmed separately.

Who should handle claim appeals in a medical practice?

This typically involves billing staff, coders, and sometimes the treating provider, depending on the complexity of the denial. Clinical documentation questions may need provider input, while coding or submission errors are often handled by billing and coding staff.

What is the difference between a corrected claim and a formal appeal?

A corrected claim fixes a clerical error, such as a wrong code or date, and is resubmitted through normal claims processing. A formal appeal disputes the payer's decision and requires a written request with supporting documentation, which is a separate process from simple claim correction.

How can practices reduce the number of denied claims over time?

Reviewing denial patterns, improving documentation practices, and verifying coding accuracy before submission can all help reduce repeat denials. Ongoing staff education and periodic internal reviews are commonly used to address recurring denial causes.

Does timely filing affect the right to appeal?

Yes, if a claim is denied for timely filing, the appeal usually needs to demonstrate that the claim was submitted within the payer's required window, often with proof of timely submission. Without that evidence, a timely filing denial can be difficult to overturn.
