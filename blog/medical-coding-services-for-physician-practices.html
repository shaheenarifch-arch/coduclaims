Quick answer

Medical coding services help a practice turn clinical documentation into accurate ICD-10-CM, CPT and HCPCS codes, and check that the record supports what is billed. For a physician practice, the most useful services are coding reviews, documentation and chart audits, denial prevention, revenue cycle consulting, compliance audits and provider education.   • Documentation audits check whether the note itself is complete enough to support the codes
  • Denial prevention finds the root causes behind your denials and fixes them upstream
  • Revenue cycle consulting maps your workflow and tracks the KPIs that show where money is lost
  • Compliance audits and provider education reduce risk and keep improvements in place

Key takeaways

  • Coding errors run in both directions: under-coding leaves earned revenue unbilled, and over-coding creates audit and repayment risk.

  • Start with a sample review so decisions are based on your own data, not on industry averages.

What are medical coding services?

Medical coding translates what happened in a patient encounter into standard code sets that payers use to process claims. Three code sets do most of the work in a physician practice:

  • ICD-10-CM describes diagnoses and the reasons for care. It is maintained in the US by the CDC's National Center for Health Statistics, with CMS.
  • CPT describes procedures and services, including evaluation and management (E/M) visits. It is published by the American Medical Association.
  • HCPCS Level II covers supplies, drugs, equipment and some services that CPT does not, and is maintained by CMS.

On top of the codes, payers apply rules: modifiers that explain special circumstances, bundling edits such as the National Correct Coding Initiative (NCCI) edits used by Medicare and many other payers, medically unlikely edits (MUEs) that cap units, and each plan's own coverage policies. Every code set is updated at least once a year, so coding that was correct last year can be wrong today.

Medical coding services are the outside help a practice uses to get this right. Some practices outsource day-to-day coding entirely. Many more keep coding in house and bring in a specialist to review accuracy, audit documentation, find denial patterns and train providers. Mass Medical Coding focuses on that second model: review, audit and improvement work that makes your existing team and billing setup produce cleaner claims. 1. Medical coding reviews

A coding review compares the codes billed for a sample of encounters with the provider's documentation and current code-set guidelines. It is the most direct way to measure coding accuracy, and it gives you a baseline you can improve against.

What a coding review covers

  • Diagnosis coding to the highest specificity the documentation supports (ICD-10-CM)
  • Procedure and E/M level selection (CPT), including medical decision making and time-based coding
  • HCPCS Level II codes for supplies, drugs and services
  • Modifier use, including 25, 59 and the XE, XP, XS and XU modifiers
  • NCCI edit and medically unlikely edit (MUE) conflicts
  • Hierarchical condition category (HCC) capture for risk-adjusted contracts

What you receive

Each review produces an accuracy score by provider and by code category, a findings report with the corrected code and the documentation reference behind it, an estimate of the revenue impact of the errors found, and practical recommendations for providers and billing staff. Because every finding comes with its reason, your team learns what to change rather than just receiving a list of corrections.

How many charts?

Sample size depends on the number of providers and your goals. A common starting point is 10 to 20 encounters per provider, agreed with you before work begins. Routine coding reviews have a standard target turnaround of 24 hours; larger audits are scheduled with an agreed delivery date before work starts. Learn more on our medical coding reviews page.

2. Documentation and chart audits

Clinical documentation drives every code you bill. A claim can be coded perfectly and still fail if the note does not show why the service was needed, or does not support the E/M level chosen. Documentation and chart audits look at that foundation.

A chart audit checks whether the billed codes match the record. A documentation review looks one step earlier, at whether the record itself is complete enough to support accurate coding. Most practices benefit from both, and Mass Medical Coding can combine them in one engagement.

Common gaps an audit finds

  • Missing or vague diagnoses that limit coding specificity
  • Medical decision making or time that does not support the billed E/M level
  • Medical necessity gaps for procedures, tests and referrals
  • Incomplete signatures, dates and authentication
  • Copy-forward and cloned notes that create compliance risk
  • Chronic conditions documented without status and plan

Results come as provider-level documentation scorecards, examples of compliant documentation for common gaps, a prioritized improvement plan and an optional follow-up audit to measure progress. Many practices audit at least once a year, and more often for new providers, new services or after a payer review. Our documentation tips for providers page lists habits that prevent the most common gaps.

3. Denial prevention

Every denied claim costs staff time and delays cash. Some denials can be corrected and resent; others need a formal appeal and may still fail. The cheapest denial is the one that never happens, which is why denial prevention focuses on the patterns behind your denials rather than on individual claims.

How denial prevention works

  1. Analyze denial and remittance data by payer, reason code, provider and service.
  2. Separate coding and documentation causes from eligibility, authorization and timely-filing causes.
  3. Recommend fixes at the point where each error starts.
  4. Build simple checklists and edits that catch errors before submission.

The data needed is usually your denial and remittance reports (835 data or a denial export from your billing system) for the last several months, which is enough to show the main patterns. Reviews account for each payer's rules across Medicare, state Medicaid programs, Medicare Advantage and commercial plans.

Mass Medical Coding's focus here is prevention and root-cause correction rather than working appeals, though we can advise on the documentation that supports an appeal. If you are working denials yourself, our guides on how to reduce claim denials and how to appeal denied insurance claims walk through both sides step by step.

4. Revenue cycle consulting

Coding is one link in a longer chain that starts when a patient books and ends when the payment is posted. Revenue cycle consulting looks at the whole chain to find where money is lost or delayed.

Where consulting helps

  • Workflow mapping from scheduling and eligibility to charge entry and payment posting
  • Charge capture and missed-charge review
  • Coding and documentation optimization
  • Payer mix and reimbursement trend reviews
  • Assessments of your billing vendor's or in-house team's performance

The KPIs worth tracking

At minimum, track four measures every month: clean claim rate (claims accepted on first submission), denial rate, days in accounts receivable and net collection rate. Compare your own numbers month to month rather than chasing outside benchmarks, because results vary widely by specialty, payer mix and region. Our revenue insights page explains what each measure tells you.

An engagement starts with a short discovery assessment, followed by findings and a prioritized plan, then implementation support for as long as you need it. Engagements can be one-time or ongoing. Consulting does not replace your billing company; it works alongside your in-house team or vendor and gives you independent visibility into results.

5. Compliance audits

Independent coding compliance audits help you find and correct risk before a payer or regulator does. Regular auditing and monitoring is one of the core elements of an effective compliance program described in the HHS Office of Inspector General's General Compliance Program Guidance, and documented audit results give you evidence you can act on and keep on file.

What a compliance audit looks at

  • E/M level distribution compared with specialty norms
  • High-risk modifiers and code pairs
  • Documentation that supports medical necessity
  • Copy-forward and cloned documentation
  • Incident-to and split/shared visit billing

Audits can cover the whole practice or focus on one provider or one service line. If you receive a records request or audit notice, Mass Medical Coding can review the requested records first so you understand your position and any corrective steps. Results are shared only with your practice. For legal questions, or if you want a review done under a privileged arrangement, consult your healthcare attorney. See our compliance audits page for details.

6. Provider education

Audit findings only pay off if habits change. Provider education turns findings into short, specialty-specific sessions that help clinicians document efficiently and code with confidence.

  • Topics: E/M documentation based on medical decision making or time, diagnosis specificity for chronic conditions and risk adjustment, procedure documentation and modifier use, medical necessity for referrals and tests, and the common audit findings in your specialty.
  • Formats: one-on-one sessions built from each provider's own audit findings, group sessions for departments or new hires, and quick-reference tip sheets for the exam room.
  • Length and delivery: most sessions run 30 to 60 minutes to fit around clinical schedules, and can be virtual or on site.

Pair education with a follow-up audit a few months later so you can see whether the changes held. Read more on our provider education page, and use our coding guidance resources between sessions.

How is patient information protected?

Any outside coding work touches patient records, so privacy terms should be settled before anything is shared. Under HIPAA, a vendor that handles protected health information (PHI) on a practice's behalf is generally a business associate, and the practice needs a Business Associate Agreement (BAA) with it. HHS publishes sample BAA provisions that show what these agreements usually cover.

Mass Medical Coding's workflows are built around HIPAA privacy and security requirements, with access limited to the staff working on your account. If an engagement requires access to patient records, we agree a secure transfer method and the appropriate agreements, including a BAA where needed, before any records are shared, and we work with de-identified or minimum-necessary records wherever possible.

How do you choose a medical coding partner?

Whoever you work with, ask these questions before you sign:

  • Who reviews the charts? Ask about the reviewers' experience in your specialty and how they keep up with annual code updates.
  • What does a finding look like? Ask for a sample report. Each finding should show the original code, the corrected code and the documentation reason.
  • How is the sample chosen? A good partner agrees sample size and selection with you before starting.
  • How are records shared? Expect a BAA where needed, a secure transfer method and minimum-necessary data.
  • Does it work with your systems? Reviews should not require you to change EHR or billing vendor.
  • What happens after the report? Look for a results walkthrough, provider feedback and an optional follow-up audit.
  • How is pricing set? Ask for a written, scoped quote rather than a vague percentage.
  • What is promised? Be cautious of guaranteed revenue increases or denial rates. Honest partners set targets and report results; they do not guarantee outcomes they do not control.

Common mistakes

  • Auditing only after a problem: waiting for a payer letter means errors have often been repeating for months.
  • Fixing codes but not documentation: corrected codes without better notes lead to the same errors next month.
  • Chasing only under-coding: a review that only looks for missed revenue ignores the over-coding that creates repayment risk.
  • Working denials one by one: without a log by reason code, the same root cause keeps returning.
  • Skipping provider feedback: findings that never reach clinicians rarely change anything.
  • Sharing records before agreements are in place: settle the BAA and transfer method first.

Limitations and checks

  • Code sets change every year. Verify coding points against current CPT, ICD-10-CM and HCPCS publications and applicable payer edits.
  • Payer rules on coverage, authorization, modifiers and filing limits differ by payer, plan and state. Confirm current requirements in each payer's provider manual.
  • The performance targets on our site are shown for illustration; actual results vary by practice, and no outside review can guarantee that a claim will be paid.
  • This article is general guidance, not legal, compliance or payer-specific advice. Consult your healthcare attorney on legal questions.
  • Share only the minimum patient information each step needs, and follow your HIPAA policies.
